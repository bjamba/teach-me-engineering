Control plane vs data plane

A small distinction that explains why Tailscale "knowing" about your devices is different from Tailscale "seeing your traffic."

1Two planes, two responsibilities

Tailscale's architecture has two layers running in parallel:

  • Control plane — Tailscale Inc.'s servers. Handles login, device management, key exchange, ACL distribution, and the NAT-traversal handshake. This is the part that knows which devices are in your tailnet and where they currently are.
  • Data plane — your devices, talking directly. The actual packets of your data travel here, encrypted with WireGuard. Tailscale Inc. is not in this path.

The aha: Tailscale Inc. coordinates connections but doesn't see your data. The control plane is metadata; the data plane is content.

2What the control plane knows

The Tailscale coordinator knows:

  • What devices are in your tailnet
  • Their public WireGuard keys
  • Their public IP and port (for the simultaneous-open trick)
  • What ACL rules apply
  • Some metadata: when devices last connected, where from

It doesn't know what you're doing with those devices, what files you're transferring, what services you're running.

3What the data plane carries

Once two devices have shaken hands, packets flow directly between them, encrypted with keys that only they know. The path looks like:

your laptop  ←──── encrypted WireGuard tunnel ────→  your home server
                       (Tailscale doesn't see this)

If direct connection fails (symmetric NAT, etc.), packets fall back to a Tailscale-operated DERP relay. Even then, the relay can't read your data — it's still encrypted end-to-end with WireGuard. The relay only forwards encrypted bytes.

4Why this matters

This separation is the answer to "is Tailscale a privacy risk?" — the company has access to connection metadata (who's in your tailnet, when they're online), but not your traffic. If you trust Tailscale not to leak the metadata (or if you want to remove that trust requirement entirely, see Headscale), you've got a private mesh network with end-to-end encryption.

It also matters operationally. If Tailscale's coordinator goes down (rare, but it has happened), existing connections keep working — devices already know each other's keys and can talk. New devices joining your tailnet would fail until the coordinator's back. So it's not a single point of failure for live traffic, only for tailnet-management actions.

Three quick questions

Q1.What does Tailscale Inc.'s control plane see about your traffic?
Right. Control plane = metadata. Data plane = your actual bytes, end-to-end encrypted between your devices.
Tailscale isn't in the data path for direct peer connections.
Even when traffic goes through a DERP relay, it's still encrypted; the relay can't read it.
Just timestamps would be wrong too — they see other metadata. The point is they don't see traffic content.
Q2.What happens to your live tailnet if Tailscale's coordinator goes offline?
Yep. The coordinator is needed for tailnet-management actions, not for live data. Devices that already know each other keep talking via WireGuard.
Existing connections persist. Only management actions break.
Encryption isn't tied to the coordinator. Your data is still encrypted.
Made up.
Q3.Why isn't a DERP relay a privacy risk?
Right. DERP is a fallback transport, not a decryption point. Your traffic is encrypted with keys only the two endpoints have.
You generally can't disable DERP fully; it's the safety net for symmetric NAT cases. And you shouldn't need to.
DERP servers are run by Tailscale Inc. The privacy comes from the encryption, not who runs the relay.
Promises are nice; cryptography is better. The encryption is what matters.
