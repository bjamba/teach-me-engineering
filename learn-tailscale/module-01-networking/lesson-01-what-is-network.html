What is a network

A network is two or more computers that can pass each other messages. That's the entire definition. Everything we'll learn is about how they manage that.

1The simplest possible network

Plug an Ethernet cable between two laptops. Configure them right and they can send each other files. That is a complete, working network — two computers, one wire.

Add a third laptop and a switch. Now all three can talk. Add wifi. Add fifty more devices. The complexity grows, but the core stays the same: computers passing messages over some kind of medium.

The aha: the internet isn't fundamentally different from your home wifi. It's the same idea, scaled up insanely, with intermediaries — but the principle ("computer A wants to send a message to computer B") never changes.

2Three sizes of network

Networking people use three terms a lot:

  • LANLAN (Local Area Network) — the network inside one home or office. Your wifi router and everything connected to it form a LAN. Devices in a LAN can usually reach each other directly. — your home or office. Everything connected to one router. Devices on the same LAN can usually reach each other directly.
  • WANWAN (Wide Area Network) — a network spanning a large geographic area. The internet itself is the largest WAN. Connecting two LANs over a WAN is what Tailscale (and VPNs) make easy. — anything bigger than your house. Two offices in different cities, connected. The internet at large is the biggest WAN.
  • Internet — capital-I, the global mesh of WANs run by ISPs and telecoms. When you "go online" you're routing packets through dozens of these networks.

The reason we name them: the rules differ. Inside a LAN, computers can usually find each other freely. Crossing a WAN, things get messy — that's where NAT, firewalls, and Tailscale enter the picture.

3Packets, the atoms of the internet

When your laptop sends data — a webpage request, a file, a Zoom video — it's broken into small chunks called packets. Each packet has:

  • A destination address (where it's going)
  • A source address (where it came from, so replies know how to come back)
  • A small payload (a fragment of the actual data)

The packet bounces from one router to the next — each router glances at the destination and forwards it toward something closer to the destination. Each step is called a hop.

Mental model: packets are postcards. Each one has the address on the front, the message on the back. A "letter" of data is just a stack of postcards mailed in sequence. The post office (the internet) delivers each card; your computer reassembles them at the other end.

Deep-path detail: what we casually call a "packet" is technically a few different things stacked up — frames at the link layer, IP packets at the network layer, TCP segments at the transport layer. You don't need to keep these straight unless you're a network engineer. The "postcard with an address" model gets you 90%.

4Wifi, Ethernet, cellular — all the same thing

At the level we care about, it doesn't matter how a packet physically travels. Wifi sends it as radio waves. Ethernet sends it as electrical pulses through copper. Cellular bounces it through cell towers. Fiber sends it as light pulses.

The packets are identical. Your laptop on wifi and your laptop on a wired connection both speak the same protocols. This is why your devices can roam between networks without anything fundamentally changing — the post office routes postcards regardless of how they were dropped off.

5Why this matters for the rest of the course

Tailscale doesn't make a "new" network. It makes a network between your devices that pretends to be a single LAN — even when those devices are scattered across LANs, WANs, and the internet at large. Knowing what a network actually is makes that promise concrete: your devices act like they're all on one cable, even when they're on different continents.

That's the whole pitch. The next five lessons unpack the specific obstacles Tailscale removes.

Usually the opposite — LANs are typically faster than WANs because they're physically smaller. But the definition isn't about speed.
